Figures that move

The three blocks that move, when each one earns its place, and what a reader who does not scroll smoothly still gets.

A figure moves only when the idea has an order. Then it holds still beside the words, and each step you scroll changes one thing in it, which the step names.

A walkthrough over a drawing

The figure below is a kit component. The walkthrough steps it by changing one attribute per step; nothing is redrawn by hand. It is also the order in which a course gets made.

  1. Intake

    Intake, first box: the learner's expertise, their gear and versions, their own names for their two rooms, and their own notes to fold in. Deduced where possible, asked where not.

  2. Research

    Research: one sourced note per topic, every claim with a page reference, and a list of what could not be confirmed. That list becomes the red flags.

  3. Plan

    Plan: one idea per lesson, its figures, at most two walkthroughs, the exercises by room, and the encore. The learner reads it before any page exists.

  4. Build

    Build, first box of the second row: identity, course data, drawings, then the lessons. The check runs every beat of every walkthrough at phone and desktop sizes.

  5. Fact-check

    Fact-check: readers who did not write the pages check every operational claim against the research. Wrong claims are fixed, unconfirmed ones are flagged, and no flag is ever removed.

  6. Publish

    Publish, last box, is optional: behind a login, never an open link, and only when the learner says yes.

One step, one change

How the stepping works, drawn with hand-made SVG instead of a component. On a phone the figure pins under the rail and the steps pass beneath it.

  1. The figure pins

    The pinned figure box sticks under the rail and keeps the same size at every beat, so the text under it never jumps.

  2. A switch line sits under it

    The dashed switch line sits a third of the way into the space left below the figure. On a wide screen it sits beside the figure instead.

  3. A step crosses it

    When the step 1 card crosses the line, the pinned figure changes. Scrolling back up undoes it: the picture depends only on which step is active.

Two states of one thing

A compare flips one figure between named states when the reader clicks. It never flips by itself. Here the two states are the two ways to read a walkthrough; the chevrons in the first state are a flow, moving only while the figure is on screen.

Why the reader sets the pace: learning improves when material is segmented and learners control the pace, and when cues signal how it is organised. Why most of a lesson stays still: extraneous material costs comprehension.

The example band

Write a beat table

  1. Pick one move from a subject you know that has a real order: three to six steps.
  2. In a plan file, write the table: beat, step headline, what changes in the figure. Beat 0 is before the first step.
  3. Strike any beat that changes two things, and any step that names its change only as "the highlighted one".

every beat changes one thing, and every step names what changed by its label in the drawing.

Read it two ways

  1. Open this lesson on a phone. Scroll the pipeline walkthrough slowly and watch the boxes light.
  2. Press Show all steps and find the Fact-check step again.

the same six stages reach you both ways, and in flat mode you find Fact-check without scrolling through the other steps one by one.
